## Claims swapped, cut or rewritten

**1. Total unspent gift card value: $21 billion → $27 billion.**
The original figure came from an earlier Bankrate survey. The current Bankrate
survey (fielded 19 to 21 August 2024, n=2,373) puts outstanding unused gift
cards, vouchers and store credit at about $27 billion, averaging $244 per
person, with 43 percent of US adults holding at least one. No government body
publishes this number, so it stays CONTESTED and the narration says
"an estimated" and explicitly calls it a survey estimate, not a government
figure.

**2. Starbucks breakage: $212 million → $222 million.**
The FY2025 Form 10-K, for the fiscal year ended 28 September 2025, discloses
breakage revenue of $200.4 million in company-operated store revenues and
$22.0 million in licensed store revenues. Those two disclosed breakage lines
sum to $222.4 million. The narration says "two hundred and twenty two million",
which rounds down and never overstates the filing.

**3. "At least nineteen states" hold the balance / "thirty seven states" let the
company keep it: CUT and rewritten.**
Two independent problems. First, the two counts sum to fifty six, which exceeds
the number of states, so the original pair could not both be true. Second, no
official body publishes a national count of states that escheat gift card
balances, and the available counts come from advisory firms that disagree with
each other. Four query reformulations failed to reach an official or primary
source. Under hard rule 6 the count was cut rather than softened.

The beat was rewritten around what is verifiable: two named states with their
own statutes on opposite sides of the question. New York deems an unredeemed
gift certificate abandoned after five years and pays it to the state
comptroller (NY Abandoned Property Law § 1315). California exempts most gift
certificates from escheat entirely (Code of Civil Procedure § 1520.5). The
narration then states plainly that there is no single national rule.

**4. "Several states require retailers to pay cash on small remaining balances"
→ "Fourteen states".**
The Connecticut General Assembly Office of Legislative Research report
2023-R-0195 lists fourteen states with gift card cash-back laws, with the
statutory citation for each. This is a state legislative research office
publication and is treated as OFFICIAL. The fourteen are California, Colorado,
Connecticut, Hawaii, Maine, Massachusetts, Montana, New Jersey, New York,
Oregon, Rhode Island, Texas, Vermont and Washington.

**5. California cash-back threshold: $9.99 → under $15.**
The OLR report above is stale on California. Senate Bill 22 amended Civil Code
§ 1749.5 so that a gift certificate with a cash value of less than fifteen
dollars is redeemable in cash. The amended section is operative from 1 April
2026 and is therefore in force as of this build. Verified against the statute
text on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov rather than the secondary summary.

## Priority rule accuracy note

The original script stated the escheat rule as "state of incorporation, not
where you live". That is the second priority rule, not the first. Under
*Texas v. New Jersey*, 379 U.S. 674 (1965), unclaimed property goes first to
the state of the owner's last known address on the holder's books, and only
falls to the holder's state of incorporation when no address is recorded.
Because a shop does not record who bought a gift card, gift card balances
usually do fall to the second rule. The expanded script states both rules in
order (beats 27 and 28) so the mechanism is correct rather than merely
memorable.

## Link verification

All URLs above were requested on 2026-07-30. Four hosts return HTTP 403 to
scripted requests because of bot protection, and were verified by other means:

- `sec.gov` returns 200 with a declared User-Agent, per SEC access policy. The
  breakage sentence was read directly from the filing.
- `consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/20/` returned full
  regulation text through the fetch tool; only raw curl is blocked.
- `missingmoney.com` blocks all automated requests. Its liveness and its
  operator are confirmed by NAUPA's own site at unclaimed.org, which states
  that NAUPA manages MissingMoney.com and that searching is free.
- `nysenate.gov` returns 403 to curl but served the full text of Abandoned
  Property Law § 1315 through the fetch tool. The dormancy period and the
  payment obligation quoted above were read from that response.

`law.cornell.edu`, `leginfo.legislature.ca.gov`, `app.leg.wa.gov`,
`cga.ct.gov`, `unclaimed.org`, `usa.gov` and `bankrate.com` all return HTTP 200
to a plain request. No official link in this episode is dead.
