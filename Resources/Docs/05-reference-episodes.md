# UNCLAIMED · REFERENCE EPISODES

The two produced episodes, verbatim. These are the gold standard. When writing a new
episode, match these on beat length, sentence rhythm, VOICE direction style, VISUAL
grammar and the shape of the turn. Do not copy their objects or figures.

Note on Episode 2: the plain Script section carries twelve timestamps because 02:30 and
02:45 were written as separate narration paragraphs, then combined into the single
20 second final beat in the production script. The production script is authoritative:
eleven beats.

# EPISODE 1 · What Happens to Gift Card Balances Nobody Spends

## Script

[00:00] There is a gift card in your house right now with money still on it. Not the whole balance. Just the awkward part you never went back for.

[00:15] Americans leave an estimated twenty one billion dollars sitting on gift cards. That money does not wait patiently for you. Inside the company that sold it, your leftover has a name.

[00:30] It is called breakage. And breakage is not a loss. It is income. When you buy a fifty dollar card, the retailer cannot count that fifty as revenue yet.

[00:45] It sits on their books as a promise they owe you in goods. You spend thirty seven of it. You mean to go back for the rest. You do not.

[01:00] The card moves to a drawer and stays there. They have the data across millions of cards. Eventually they stop calling your thirteen dollars a promise and start calling it revenue.

[01:15] Starbucks alone reported two hundred and twelve million dollars of breakage in a single year. That is money customers paid for coffee they never drank, counted as profit.

[01:30] But in at least nineteen states that is not allowed. Unclaimed property law says the company never earned it, so the balance goes to the state instead, held for you.

[01:45] Thirty seven states go the other way. California, Texas, Florida, Illinois. There the company simply keeps it. Legally, permanently, and by design. It depends on where they are incorporated.

[02:00] Not where you live. And where a state does hold it, that money is searchable by name. Go to MissingMoney dot com. It is free and it covers most states.

[02:15] Search every version of your name, and every old address. Records follow the address the company had, not the one you have now. Misspellings and maiden names count too.

[02:30] Then check the drawer. Several states require retailers to pay cash on small remaining balances if you ask. Most people never ask. The money may be unclaimed. It does not have to stay that way.

# EPISODE 2 · Disaster Relief Money That Goes Unclaimed Every Year

## Script

[00:00] After a disaster, money is set aside for the people it happened to. Not a loan. A grant. And a large share of it is never collected.

[00:15] Not because it ran out. Because nobody claimed it. Aid does not arrive automatically. Somebody has to apply, inside a window, with documents that may have just been destroyed.

[00:30] So the people who need it most are the ones least able to chase it. And the window is short. Applications usually close about sixty days after the declaration.

[00:45] But the bigger loss happens later, to people who did apply. They get a letter saying they are ineligible. They read it as a no. They stop.

[01:00] Here is the part almost nobody knows. FEMA has said publicly that a letter saying you are ineligible may not be a denial at all. Very often, they just need more information.

[01:15] Missing proof you lived there. Missing proof you owned it. An insurance decision they have not seen yet. Fixable things, described in a letter most people never finish reading.

[01:30] You have sixty days from the date on that decision letter to appeal. Many people who were turned down at first do receive assistance once they appeal.

[01:45] The letter contains a denial code. Those codes are explained in a free FEMA publication called Help After a Disaster. The code tells you exactly what is missing.

[02:00] You can also request a copy of your own file. It includes the inspector's report and every call logged. That is often where you find the mistake.

[02:15] If you never applied at all, check disasterassistance.gov, or call the FEMA helpline. Deadlines are sometimes extended, and there are other programmes running alongside the main one.

[02:30] And if the sixty days have already passed, write anyway, and explain why it was late. It is called a reconsideration, and it has no firm deadline.

[02:45] Most people who were owed something stopped at a letter they thought was final. It usually was not. The money may be unclaimed. It does not have to stay that way.
