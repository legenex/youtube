# CHANNEL 1: UNCLAIMED

## Positioning

Unclaimed reveals money, benefits, compensation, refunds, settlements and financial rights that ordinary people do not know exist.

Every episode opens on the same possibility: there may be money connected to your name, your situation, or something that happened to you.

Not a personal injury channel. Coverage spans unclaimed property, insurance benefits, class action settlements, employment compensation, consumer refunds, government programmes, tax credits, banking errors, travel compensation, property deposits, estates and inheritance, data breach settlements, product recalls and medical billing refunds.

**Central question:** is money being held, withheld, overlooked or left unclaimed?

## Why it separates from every other money channel

Finance channels cover earning, investing, saving, side hustles and getting rich. Unclaimed covers money that may already be attached to the viewer. That produces a stronger curiosity gap ("could this apply to me") and it lands directly on existing search behaviour, since people already search unclaimed money, refund eligibility, settlement eligibility, compensation rules, insurance benefits, class action payments, government credits, missing inheritance and consumer rights.

## Editorial boundaries

**Does:** explain what the money is, why it exists, who may qualify, where it is held, what the process looks like, what proof is usually needed, what mistakes prevent recovery, and where official information can be checked.

**Does not:** guarantee eligibility, guarantee payment, promise a settlement value, imply every viewer has money waiting, use manufactured urgency, present general information as personalised legal, tax or financial advice, or turn an episode into a lead form.

The funnel stays separate from the educational answer. This is the rule that protects the channel's entire value.

## Audience

US adults 25 to 65. Homeowners, employees, parents, consumers, travellers, accident victims, former tenants, people managing estates, people dealing with insurance or medical bills.

Secondary: finance and consumer-rights viewers, older viewers, family members helping relatives, people who distrust complex institutions, people under sudden financial pressure.

## Format

Second person. Eight to twelve minutes. Search-led, packaged for suggested traffic. Calm, credible, practical. No "free money" language. Every episode ends with a verification step.

## Episode structure

1. **The overlooked money.** State the opportunity plainly. "Every year, security deposits are withheld from tenants who could have challenged the deduction."
2. **Why the money exists.** The programme, obligation, settlement, policy or legal mechanism.
3. **Who may qualify.** Eligibility branches shown visually.
4. **Where people lose it.** Missed deadlines, missing documents, wrong assumptions, scams, incomplete submissions.
5. **How to verify it.** Official databases, notices, administrators, documentation. Named on screen, never in the description alone.
6. **What to prepare.** A simple document checklist.
7. **Sign-off.** "The money may be unclaimed. It does not have to stay that way."

## Design angle

**Financial investigation drawn as a map.** The visual thesis is tracing where money went.

- White or light paper ground, subtle fibre texture
- Black marker linework, slightly imperfect
- Green or gold as the single accent
- Recurring frame objects: claim forms, letters, payment trails, timelines, document stacks, portal screens, eligibility trees, money moving between institutions
- One highlighted amount or one missing step per frame, never two

**The accent rule.** Green marks money that is recoverable. Grey marks money that is gone. That binary is the channel's entire colour logic and it teaches itself within three episodes.

**What it must not look like:** a crypto channel, a get-rich-quick channel, a law firm advert, a government presentation, or a personal finance slideshow.

**Motion.** Elements draw on in sequence. Arrows travel along the money trail. The highlighted amount lands last in each frame. No camera moves beyond a slow drift.

**Thumbnails.** One object plus one number or one eligibility trigger. Unopened envelope, blank-name cheque, suitcase and cancelled flight, hospital bill with one circled charge, house and deposit receipt, settlement notice, old bank account, highlighted deadline. Four words maximum: They Still Owe You / Check Your Name / You May Qualify / Refund Never Sent / Where It Went / Before It Expires.

## How to create the videos

1. **Topic selection** from verified consumer search demand, not from interest.
2. **Research official sources first.** State treasury sites, settlement administrator pages, court dockets, agency refund programmes. Structured public record, which is why this channel automates well.
3. **Build the eligibility and process map** before writing a word. The map is the episode; the script narrates it.
4. **Script** in the locked second-person format.
5. **Fact-check every amount, deadline and qualification.** Hard gate: no claim ships without a live official URL in the script file.
6. **Produce 100 to 140 hand-drawn frames** against the verbatim style string.
7. **Screen-record the official portals** and cut those in directly. Never recreate a government portal as an illustration, since the real thing is both clearer and the stronger trust signal.
8. **Animate** through reveals, travelling arrows and highlighted documents.
9. **Disclaimer and official links** in a fixed description block.
10. **One long-form plus three Shorts,** cut from the "how to check" beat.
11. **Mine comments** for the next high-intent question. This channel's comment section is a topic pipeline.

# CHANNEL 2: BEHAVIOR BY DESIGN

## Positioning

Behavior by Design explains the hidden wiring behind what people do, and the systems built around that wiring.

It merges the two earlier concepts. Wired by Default supplied the instincts, adaptations and mental systems inside people. By Design supplied the environments, products, interfaces and commercial systems built to influence them.

**Unifying thesis:** your reaction may be human. The setup may be intentional.

American spelling on the name, since the commercial audience is US.

## Central question

Every episode answers one of two:

- **Internal:** why do people naturally do this?
- **Crossover:** what human tendency is being activated, and how was the system designed around it?

## Editorial split

**Family A, Built into you.** Psychology, evolution, attention, memory, social behaviour, perception.

**Family B, Built around you.** Retail design, pricing, software, interfaces, offices, airports, subscriptions, institutions.

Long-term balance around 50/50. That split is what prevents the channel becoming generic psychology facts, a dark-pattern outrage channel, a business breakdown clone, or a conspiracy channel. Each of those four is a failure mode the split actively blocks.

## Tone

Calm, mechanical, curious, dry, non-moralising. No outrage narrator. No "companies are evil" simplification. No fake certainty about contested psychology.

The narrator sounds like a person with a marker explaining a machine.

## Audience

18 to 44. Psychology and science viewers, business and design viewers, productivity audiences, technology users, explainer and curiosity channel viewers. High international potential because the mechanisms are universal, which is also what caps the RPM.

## Format

Second person. Nine to thirteen minutes. Roughly 1,600 to 2,100 words. Strong recognition hook. One core mechanism per episode. One practical reframe or tell at the end.

## Episode structure

1. **Recognition.** State something the viewer does. "You lower the music when you are looking for an address."
2. **The obvious explanation.** What people normally assume, dismissed quickly.
3. **The mechanism.** What is actually happening in attention, memory, social processing or perception.
4. **The original function.** What the mechanism helped people do.
5. **The modern trade-off.** Where it misfires now.
6. **The designed layer.** On crossover episodes, how an app, shop, interface or institution uses that tendency.
7. **The red mark.** The exact point where natural behaviour becomes directed behaviour.
8. **The tell.** How to notice it next time.
9. **Sign-off.** Crossover: "Your reaction was human. The setup was by design." Pure psychology: "You are not malfunctioning. You are behaving by design."

## Design angle

**Diagram-first sketch animation on faint graph paper.**

- Faint grid ground, always visible, never dominant
- Monochrome hand-drawn linework
- Hand-drawn boxes and arrows
- Simplified stick figures inherited from the doodle style: large circular head, dot eyes, thick brow lines
- Chunky flat single-colour objects
- Interface mock-ups, isometric physical spaces, flowcharts, behaviour loops
- One controlled red highlight per frame

**The red rule.** Red marks only the behavioural trigger, the design choice, the moment of manipulation, the hidden incentive, or the mechanism itself. Never decorative. Within three episodes viewers read red as "this is the point" before the narrator says it. This single rule is the channel's whole visual identity and it is the cheapest brand asset in the network.

**Motion.** Progressive drawing. Elements appear in sequence as the narrator reaches them, then the red mark lands. That beat is the payoff of every segment and it is trivially cheap to produce. Only a handful of genuinely moving sequences per episode.

**Thumbnails.** One object or figure, one red mark, one short phrase, four words maximum, large negative space. No collages, no exaggerated faces. WHY YOU FREEZE / THE MISSING BOTTOM / YOU NEVER CHOSE / THE THIRD OPTION / BUILT TO DISTRACT / YOUR BRAIN NOTICED.

**The style string is non-negotiable.** Every image prompt reuses the exact character construction and style language verbatim, never a paraphrase. Prompt drift off that string is what caused the multipotentiality frames to fail, and it is the single cheapest production failure to prevent.

## How to create the videos

1. Select one behaviour or one designed outcome.
2. Identify the strongest research question behind it.
3. Gather primary or high-quality sources.
4. Separate established findings from speculation explicitly in the script file. Contested findings get flagged as contested on screen.
5. Write against the locked format.
6. Generate 110 to 150 timestamped frames.
7. Inject the visual style language programmatically into every prompt so no agent can paraphrase it.
8. Animate through progressive drawing.
9. Use only a few genuine moving sequences per episode.
10. Cut Shorts from the recognition beat and the red-mark beat. Those two beats are where this format's viral potential actually lives.

## RPM and CPM

**RPM $5 to $13. CPM $10 to $26.**

Higher on episodes involving money, software, business and consumer decisions, which is the Built Around You half. Lower on broad psychology and internationally distributed topics, which is the Built Into You half. The 50/50 split is therefore also a revenue decision, not only an editorial one.

# CHANNEL 3: LAW, ACTUALLY

## Positioning

Law, Actually explains what the law really does when ordinary life becomes legally complicated.

Not a claims channel, and deliberately not built around personal injury lead flow. Coverage spans criminal law, family law, divorce, custody, consumer law, employment, property, contracts, police powers, civil liability, inheritance, court procedure, notable legal outcomes and counterintuitive rules.

**Central promise:** people think the law works one way. Here is how it actually works.

## Separation from Unclaimed

Unclaimed asks whether there is money you may be entitled to recover. Law, Actually asks what happens legally when a situation occurs.

- Unclaimed: can you recover compensation after an accident? · Law, Actually: what actually makes a shop legally responsible when someone slips?
- Unclaimed: what happens to an inheritance nobody claims? · Law, Actually: who inherits when someone dies without a will?

Subjects may touch. The editorial question never does.

## Audience

US adults 20 to 60. True crime viewers, documentary audiences, people interested in courts and justice, people experiencing divorce, arrest, eviction, disputes or employment problems, and viewers who enjoy counterintuitive systems.

Must be understandable with zero legal background.

## Format

Second person or scenario-led. Eight to twelve minutes. One legal problem per episode. Real-world examples. Plain language. State differences acknowledged explicitly. General information only. Human legal review before publication, without exception.

## Tone

Calm, precise, slightly dry, non-partisan. Not sensational. No courtroom gravitas performance. No pretending the law is always fair. No claiming one rule applies identically in every state.

## Episode structure

1. **The situation.** "You slip on a wet supermarket floor and break your wrist."
2. **What people assume.** "The shop has insurance, so most people assume the shop automatically pays."
3. **What the law asks.** The issue broken into tests: duty, knowledge, intent, reasonableness, evidence, causation, damages, procedure.
4. **The fact that changes everything.** The detail that determines direction.
5. **What happens next.** The practical process, drawn.
6. **Why the outcome may feel wrong.** The gap between factual guilt, legal proof, procedure and enforceable rights. This beat is the channel's whole reason for existing.
7. **What remains in the person's control.** Documents, deadlines, questions, when to get professional advice.
8. **Sign-off.** "That is not what people expect the law to do. It is what the law actually does."

## Design angle

**Whiteboard legal explanation.**

- White ground, black marker linework
- One accent: blue or amber, used for the decisive fact only
- Recurring frame objects: timelines, evidence boards, courtroom layouts, decision trees, property diagrams, family structures, contracts, police encounter flowcharts
- Scales used sparingly, and only where they mean something
- One decisive fact highlighted per scene

**Avoid entirely:** gavels, wigs, generic courthouse stock imagery, dramatic lighting. Whiteboard reads as someone explaining something. Slick animation reads as advertising, and in legal that triggers immediate scepticism.

**Motion.** Draw-on as the narrator reaches each element. This holds retention unusually well here because the viewer waits to see which branch of the decision tree gets drawn next.

**Thumbnails.** One legal situation, one contradiction. Handcuffs beside a phone, a wet supermarket floor, two houses split by a boundary, a custody calendar, a signed contract, a breathalyser, a dog lead, a jury box, a will missing a signature, a dismissed evidence folder. Five words maximum: GUILTY BUT FREE / THE SHOP KNEW / POLICE GOT IT WRONG / WHO GETS THE HOUSE? / YOU ALREADY AGREED / THE EVIDENCE VANISHED / WHY HE WALKED / CAN THEY SEARCH IT?

## How to create the videos

1. Select a real-world legal question.
2. Decide the jurisdictional framing before research begins.
3. Research statutes, court rules, official guides and reported decisions.
4. Identify where state law diverges, and say so on screen rather than in a disclaimer.
5. Write in plain language.
6. **Legal review gate.** Nothing publishes without it. This is a liability exposure, not a credibility one.
7. Build the visual process map.
8. Generate and animate 100 to 140 frames.
9. Disclaimer and source block appended.
10. Cut Shorts around the counterintuitive rule, which is this channel's most shareable beat.

## Legal and platform discipline

Present general legal information. Avoid personalised advice. Avoid guaranteed outcomes. State when laws differ by state. Avoid exploiting active tragedies. Avoid graphic imagery. Keep thumbnails restrained on violent or sensitive cases. Disclose sponsors and commercial relationships clearly.

YouTube currently permits monetisation for many educational and documentary treatments of controversial or sensitive subjects where the treatment is non-graphic and non-exploitative. Graphic, exploitative or sensational treatment can still reduce or remove ad eligibility. The restraint in the tone section is therefore also a monetisation decision.

Higher on employment, contracts, insurance and consumer law, which sit in the $12 to $30 band on their own. Lower on broad crime, court stories and internationally distributed content, which is what pulls the channel average below a pure consumer-legal channel.

# AUTOMATION BLUEPRINT

The architecture that makes three channels cost roughly what one costs.

## The core principle

**One codebase. Three config files.** The pipeline is channel-agnostic. Everything channel-specific lives in a config: format bible, verbatim style string, palette, accent colour rule, voice ID, thumbnail template ID, playlist map, disclaimer block, channel ID.

Adding a fourth channel later is a config file, not a build.

## Layer by layer

**1. Demand and idea generation**
Hermes runs scheduled agents. For Unclaimed, monitoring settlement administrator feeds, court dockets and agency refund announcements. For Law, Actually, monitoring notable decisions and rule changes. For Behavior by Design, monitoring research publication feeds and consumer-interface changes. Output lands in a queue with a demand score attached.
*Automated. Human picks from the queue.*

**2. Research**
Gemini Ultra deep research against the queued topic, with a hard requirement that every factual claim carries a source URL in the output. ChatGPT as a second pass for angle expansion.
*Automated. Output is unusable without the URL requirement enforced at the schema level.*

**3. Fact gate**
A separate model instance reviews the research adversarially: which claims lack a source, which sources are secondary, which are contested. Produces a pass or fail with a reason list.
*Automated for Behavior by Design. Human sign-off mandatory for Unclaimed and Law, Actually.*

**4. Script**
Claude writes against the format bible for that channel. The bible defines beat structure, word count, second-person rules, sign-off line and disclaimer language.
*Automated.*

**5. Scene breakdown and prompt generation**
Claude Code splits the script into 100 to 150 timestamped scenes and generates an image prompt per scene. **The style string is injected programmatically from the config, never written by the model.** This is the single most important line in the whole blueprint, because the one production failure that has already happened was prompt drift off a paraphrased style string.
*Fully automated.*

**6. Image generation**
Higgsfield or Gemini against the injected prompts. Magnific upscale pass where linework needs cleaning.
*Automated. Human spot-checks a sample of ten frames per episode before assembly.*

**7. Voice**
ElevenLabs, locked voice ID per channel. Three distinctly different voices across the three channels, non-negotiable, since a shared voice is the fastest way to get algorithmically linked as one operator running a network.
*Fully automated.*

**8. Assembly**
An editor timeline built from the scene JSON: frame in, draw-on reveal, accent mark lands, frame out, timed to the voice track. Codex writes the glue scripts. Screen recordings for Unclaimed portal segments are the only manual insert.
*Automated with a manual insert step on Unclaimed.*

**9. Music and sound**
Envato library, one locked track family per channel. Minimal, no build-and-drop.
*Automated.*

**10. Thumbnails**
Canva templates, one per channel, driven by config. Object slot, accent mark slot, text slot with the word cap enforced.
*Automated. Human picks between three generated variants.*

**11. Metadata and SEO**
Title variants, description block, disclaimer block, tags, playlist assignment, end screen and card config, all generated from the channel config.
*Automated.*

**12. Publish and Shorts extraction**
Long-form upload plus three Shorts cut from the designated beats: "how to check" on Unclaimed, recognition and red-mark on Behavior by Design, the counterintuitive rule on Law, Actually.
*Automated. Human approves the publish.*

**13. Analytics loop**
Weekly pull. Click rate against the channel baseline, retention curve shape, search versus suggested impression split, Shorts-to-subscriber conversion. Feeds the retitle and rethumbnail queue.
*Automated reporting. Human decides what gets changed.*

## Where automation stops, and why

Ideation, drafting, voice, image generation, assembly, metadata and upload can all run unattended.

Four things cannot, and they are precisely what a channel-level policy review examines: fact-checking, legal review, title and thumbnail iteration, and genuine format variation between episodes. A channel that automates all four is the exact pattern being enforced against. A channel that keeps them human stays monetisable.

## Build sequence for the pipeline itself

Claude Code writes the orchestrator and the scene-breakdown service. Codex handles glue scripts, API wrappers and the assembly automation. Hermes runs the scheduled agents and the publish cadence. The first channel built pays for the pipeline; the second and third are config.

# NETWORK-LEVEL STRATEGY

## Shared infrastructure

Research orchestration, script templates, fact-checking agents, prompt generation, voice production, Canva templates, asset management, editing, Shorts extraction, upload preparation, analytics reporting, sponsor CRM, newsletter operations.

## What must not be shared

| | Unclaimed | Behavior by Design | Law, Actually |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | White paper, fibre texture | Faint graph paper | Whiteboard |
| Linework | Black marker | Monochrome sketch | Black marker |
| Accent | Green (recoverable) / grey (gone) | Red (the point) | Blue or amber (the decisive fact) |
| Frame vocabulary | Forms, letters, money trails, portals | Behaviour loops, interfaces, spaces | Evidence boards, timelines, decision trees |
| Voice | Warm, measured | Dry, mechanical | Precise, slightly cold |
| Google account | Separate | Separate | Separate |

Three distinct voices and three separate accounts. This is the network's main structural risk control.

## Launch order

Month 1: Behavior by Design. Best chance of suggested-feed growth and viral Shorts, so it proves the pipeline fastest.
Month 2: Unclaimed. Strongest direct commercial relationship and high-value search traffic.
Month 3: Law, Actually. Broadest long-term media potential, but it needs the strongest review process in place first.

Staggering prevents the same team establishing three production systems at once.

## Minimum launch inventory per channel

Three completed videos. Seven additional fact-checked scripts. Ten thumbnail concepts. Thirty Shorts hooks. Channel description. Banner. About page. Disclaimer block. Source structure. Sponsor one-sheet. Newsletter capture page.

## Cadence once all three operate

- Behavior by Design: three long-form weekly
- Unclaimed: two to three long-form weekly
- Law, Actually: two long-form weekly, rising only when legal review is stable

Total seven to eight long-form and roughly 20 Shorts weekly. Achievable only once the automated pipeline is running.

## YPP thresholds

Standard long-form route: 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 valid public watch hours in the previous 12 months. Shorts route: 1,000 subscribers plus 10 million valid public Shorts views in 90 days. Some fan-funding and Shopping features unlock earlier in eligible regions.

Shorts are a discovery tool here. Shorts Feed watch time does not count toward the 4,000-hour long-form threshold.

## Comparative rankings

**Fastest direct revenue:** Unclaimed, Law Actually, Behavior by Design
**Broadest audience growth:** Behavior by Design, Law Actually, Unclaimed
**Best sponsor pool:** Behavior by Design, Unclaimed, Law Actually
**Strongest Legenex connection:** Unclaimed, Law Actually, Behavior by Design
**Best international expansion:** Behavior by Design, Law Actually, Unclaimed

## Final assessment

Unclaimed is the commercial engine. Behavior by Design is the audience-growth engine. Law, Actually is the authority and evergreen-library engine.

**What am I owed?** · **Why did I do that?** · **What happens legally now?**
